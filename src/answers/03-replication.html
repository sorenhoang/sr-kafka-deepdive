Q1-Q3 · Junior tier, condensed

The ISR is the set of replicas currently keeping up with the leader — a follower stays in while it has fetched to the leader's log end offset within replica.lag.time.max.ms (30 s). It is not the replica list, and it moves continuously; almost every durability question reduces to how large it was at the moment of failure.

acks: 0 does not wait, so a network blip loses records with no retry possible. 1 returns once the leader has written to its own log, so a leader failure before replication loses an acknowledged write silently. all returns once every replica in the ISR has it. Since Kafka 3.0 the producer defaults are acks=all and enable.idempotence=true (KIP-679).

replication.factor=3 gives three copies on three brokers — one leader serving all traffic, two followers fetching. It lets the cluster lose a broker and still have a choice of replica to promote. On its own it guarantees nothing about data loss: a replica that exists but has fallen out of the ISR protects nothing.

Q4-Q6 · Mid tier, condensed

High watermark — the minimum log end offset across the ISR, and the furthest point consumers may read. A record only on the leader is not durable; serving it would let a consumer read something a failover could take away, making the log appear to go backwards. The cost is that a record becomes readable one replication round-trip after it is acknowledged.

Why acks=all is not enough — "all" means all replicas in the ISR. When followers drop out, the ISR is the leader alone and acks=all is satisfied by it, silently becoming acks=1 at the worst possible moment. min.insync.replicas=2 converts that into a rejected write (NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS).

Replicas: 2,3,1 · Isr: 2,3 with min ISR 2 — broker 1 has fallen behind and been evicted, so the partition is under-replicated. Writes still succeed because the ISR equals the floor, but there is no margin: one more failure and acks=all producers start failing. Broker 2 is the preferred leader and currently holds leadership. A warning, not an outage.

Q7 · Every configuration that can lose an acknowledged write

1 · acks=1. The leader acknowledges before any follower has the record, then dies. A follower is promoted, the record never existed there, and the producer was told it succeeded. Silent by construction.

2 · acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1. The dangerous one, because the configuration looks correct. The ISR shrinks to the leader alone and acks=all is satisfied by a single replica — identical exposure to case 1, reached through a setting that reads as safe in a review.

3 · Unclean leader election enabled. The whole ISR is lost, a stale replica is promoted, and every record past its log end offset is permanently gone. Consumers that already read those offsets see the log move backwards — the only case here where a consumer can observe the loss directly.

4 · Correlated power loss across the ISR. Kafka does not fsync per record; durability is replication, not flushing. If every replica sits in one rack, one availability zone or one power domain and they lose power together, data that every replica acknowledged can be gone. This is the case people omit, and it is the reason broker.rack and rack-aware placement exist.

The intended configuration is RF 3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all, unclean election off, replicas spread across racks. What it expresses is a single principle: Kafka's durability story is to refuse writes rather than lose them. Every setting above either upholds that or deliberately opts out of it, and framing the answer that way is what turns a list into a position.

Q8 · The whole ISR is gone and one stale replica survives

Two options and no third. Offline (unclean.leader.election.enable=false, the default): no acknowledged write is lost, and the partition is unavailable until an in-sync replica returns — possibly until one specific dead broker is physically recovered, which can be hours. Promote the stale replica (true): writes resume immediately, every record beyond its log end offset is permanently gone, and consumers that read those offsets see the log go backwards.

No setting avoids the trade; this is the clearest CAP choice Kafka exposes.

The decision is per topic class, and naming the dimension is what makes it a senior answer: does a downstream consumer treat this log as a source of truth? Clickstream, metrics, application logs — a gap is survivable, downtime is not, so enable it. A payment ledger, an event-sourced aggregate, anything another system reconciles against — never, take the outage and escalate. "It depends" without naming that dimension is not an answer.

Two things to add if pressed. The choice should be made per topic at creation time, not cluster-wide under incident pressure, because under pressure availability always wins the argument. And if you are reaching for unclean election regularly, the real problem is replica placement: a partition whose whole ISR can vanish at once was not spread across failure domains.

Q9 · Why leader epoch was introduced

When a failed replica rejoins it may hold records the new leader never had, so it must truncate before it can follow — the question is to what offset.

Originally it truncated to its own high watermark. The HW propagates to followers one fetch round-trip after it advances on the leader, so a follower's idea of the HW is always slightly stale. During a double failure — leader dies, a follower with a stale HW is promoted, the old leader returns — the two replicas could disagree about what had been committed. The outcomes were real: committed records silently lost, or two logs diverging at the same offsets while both replicas believed they were in sync, which is worse because nothing detects it.

Leader epoch (KIP-101, Kafka 0.11) replaced the guess with a fact. Every leader election increments an epoch number, and each replica keeps a leader-epoch-checkpoint file mapping each epoch to the offset at which it began — the file visible in any partition directory. A recovering follower asks the leader where epoch N ended and truncates to exactly that offset. Deterministic, and divergence becomes impossible rather than unlikely.

It is not configurable and needs no operational attention. It is worth knowing because it is the honest answer to "has Kafka ever lost data in normal operation" — yes, before 0.11, through precisely this path. Being able to say that, and say what fixed it, reads as someone who has read the design rather than the marketing.

Q10 · UnderMinIsrPartitionCount is 4 and producers are erroring

Read the signal first. The ISR has fallen below min.insync.replicas on four partitions, so acks=all producers are being rejected with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS. The cluster is doing exactly what it was configured to do: refusing writes it cannot make durable. The errors are a symptom; the incident is the under-replication.

Split the diagnosis in two. Are brokers down, or alive but lagging? OfflinePartitionsCount and broker liveness answer the first; IsrShrinksPerSec answers the second. The two have completely different causes and fixes, and guessing wrong wastes the first ten minutes.

  • Brokers down → how many, and are the affected partitions' replicas concentrated on them? That points at replica placement rather than at the failure.
  • Alive but lagging → the follower fetch loop is behind. Usual causes: a GC pause, a saturated network link, a disk that has silently slowed, or one broker holding a disproportionate share of leadership after an earlier failover and now saturating.

The wrong instinct, and it is worth saying out loud. Lowering min.insync.replicas stops the errors immediately — and converts a loud failure into a silent one, accepting under-replicated writes during the exact window where a second failure loses them. It is the most tempting action on the page and the one to refuse. Mitigate by restoring replicas.

Afterwards. Check leadership distribution: leaders stay where a failover put them, so recovery is not finished when brokers return. auto.leader.rebalance.enable fixes it eventually; kafka-leader-election.sh --election-type preferred fixes it now. Then ask why the ISR could shrink that far at all — if these partitions share a rack or a disk, the placement is the finding, not the lag.
