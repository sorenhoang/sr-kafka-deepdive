Module 6

Consumer internals

Where most real Kafka incidents actually happen. Producers fail loudly; consumers fail by quietly rebalancing in a loop, reprocessing everything, or stalling on a message nobody notices.

The poll loop is not what it looks like

poll() does not fetch on demand. A background fetcher keeps requests in flight to the brokers leading the assigned partitions and fills a local buffer; poll() hands you records from that buffer and returns. If the buffer has data, poll() returns immediately without any network call.

Since 0.10.1 heartbeats are on their own background thread, which is the fact the rest of this module hangs on: liveness and progress became two separate things, with two separate timeouts, and confusing them causes most consumer incidents.

max.poll.records is the one people reach for when processing is slow, and it is usually the right lever — not because it fetches less, but because it bounds how long one poll()-to-poll() cycle takes, which is what the timeout below actually measures.

The three timeouts, and the one that evicts you

SettingDefaultMeasures
heartbeat.interval.ms3 sHow often the background thread pings the coordinator
session.timeout.ms45 sNo heartbeat for this long → the process is considered dead
max.poll.interval.ms5 minNo poll() for this long → the application is considered stuck

The distinction is the whole point. Heartbeats come from a background thread, so a consumer whose processing is wedged keeps heartbeating happily — the session timeout never fires. max.poll.interval.ms exists precisely to catch that: an application that is alive but not making progress.

(session.timeout.ms was raised from 10 s to 45 s in Kafka 3.0, KIP-735, because the old value evicted healthy consumers during ordinary GC pauses and cloud network blips. Quoting 10 s dates an answer.)

The rebalance storm, step by step

Processing slows — a downstream service degrades, a batch is unusually large. One consumer exceeds max.poll.interval.ms and is evicted. The group rebalances, so every consumer stops. Partitions are reassigned, and work since the last commit is reprocessed, which makes everyone slower. The next consumer exceeds the interval. Another rebalance. The group now spends more time rebalancing than consuming and never recovers on its own.

The fix is almost never a bigger timeout. It is max.poll.records down so each cycle is shorter and bounded, plus finding out why processing slowed. Raising max.poll.interval.ms to 30 minutes makes a stuck consumer take 30 minutes to be noticed.

Offsets: where progress actually lives

Committed offsets go to __consumer_offsets — 50 partitions, compacted, keyed by (group, topic, partition). Compaction is why it converges to current position per group, and the M2 lesson applies directly. The partition a group's offsets land in also determines its group coordinator: the broker leading that partition.

A committed offset means "the next record I want", not "the last one I processed". Off-by-one here reprocesses or skips exactly one record per commit, forever.

StrategyBehaviourFailure mode
enable.auto.commit=true (default)Commits the last returned offsets every auto.commit.interval.ms (5 s), during poll()Commits what was returned, not what was processed. A crash loses up to 5 s of processing — at-least-once with a window you did not choose
commitSync()Blocks until the coordinator acknowledgesCorrect and slow; a commit per batch adds a round-trip to every cycle
commitAsync()Fire and forget with a callbackFast, but a failed commit is not retried — pair it with a commitSync() in the shutdown path

Offsets also expire: offsets.retention.minutes (7 days). A group idle longer than that loses its position and falls back to auto.offset.reset — which, on latest, silently skips everything that arrived meanwhile. Seasonal and batch consumers hit this.

auto.offset.reset applies in exactly two situations: no committed offset for the group, or the committed offset is out of range because retention deleted it (M2, step 4). earliest reprocesses, latest skips, none throws. latest is the dangerous default because its failure is silent.

Rebalance protocols — three generations

A group coordinator on one broker tracks membership. When it changes, the group rebalances. How it rebalances has been rewritten twice.

Eager — the original

Every consumer revokes all its partitions, rejoins, and waits for a new assignment computed by one elected consumer (the group leader) and distributed through the coordinator. Nothing is consumed during the join barrier. Assignment itself is client-side, which means the assignment logic lives in every client in every language. RangeAssignor and RoundRobinAssignor work this way.

Cooperative sticky — 2.4, KIP-429

CooperativeStickyAssignor makes rebalances incremental: consumers keep partitions that are not moving and revoke only the ones being reassigned, across two short rounds instead of one global stop. Adding a consumer to a healthy group stops being a cluster-wide pause. Still client-side assignment.

KIP-848 — the new consumer group protocol, GA in 4.0

Assignment moves to the broker. There is no elected group leader, no client-side assignor, and no join barrier where everyone waits. Consumers reconcile their own assignment asynchronously against what the coordinator publishes, so membership changes stop being stop-the-world at the protocol level rather than by convention. Enable with group.protocol=consumer.

Two consequences worth naming: assignment logic no longer has to be reimplemented per client language, which was a real source of cross-client inconsistency; and rebalance behaviour becomes a broker-side property you can reason about centrally instead of a property of whichever client version each team deployed.

Static membership — 2.3, KIP-345

Give each consumer a stable group.instance.id and a restart within session.timeout.ms does not trigger a rebalance — the returning member reclaims its partitions. This is what makes rolling deploys of a large consumer group survivable; without it, a 20-pod rollout is 20 rebalances.

Rebalance listeners, and the commit that must happen

  • onPartitionsRevoked — fires before partitions are taken. This is where you commit. Skip it and everything since the last commit is reprocessed by whoever gets the partition.
  • onPartitionsAssigned — after the new assignment; initialise or seek here.
  • onPartitionsLost — the partitions are already gone (the member was evicted), so committing is pointless and may fail. Clean up, do not commit.

Distinguishing revoked from lost is the detail that marks someone who has debugged this: committing in the wrong one of those two is a bug that only appears during incidents.

Reading consumer lag honestly

Lag is log end offset − committed offset. High lag means one of several very different things:

  • The consumer is genuinely slower than the producer — scale out, up to the partition count (M1).
  • A producer burst; the consumer is fine and will catch up.
  • The consumer is rebalancing repeatedly and barely consuming — lag plus a rising rebalance rate.
  • Partitions are unassigned because the group has no members at all.
  • Lag is concentrated in one partition — a hot key, not a slow consumer, and adding consumers will not help.

Check lag per partition before concluding anything. "Lag is high, add consumers" is wrong in four of those five cases.
