Module 3

Replication & ISR

The durability module. Everything here is one question asked in different ways: under which failures does Kafka lose an acknowledged write, and which settings move that line. This is where acks=all stops being a magic word.

Leaders, followers, and a fetch loop

Each partition has one leader and replication.factor - 1 followers. All produce and consume traffic goes to the leader. Followers are not pushed to — they run a fetch loop against the leader exactly like a consumer does, using the same protocol. There is no separate replication channel, which is a large part of why replication costs so little to implement and reason about.

Two offsets per replica matter:

  • LEO (log end offset) — the offset the next record will be written at. Every replica has its own.
  • HW (high watermark) — on the leader, the minimum LEO across all in-sync replicas. This is the furthest point consumers are allowed to read.
Why consumers cannot read past the high watermark

A record that only exists on the leader is not yet durable: if the leader dies, a follower that never received it becomes the new leader and the record is gone. Serving it would mean a consumer could read something that later ceases to exist — the log would appear to go backwards. Holding reads at the HW means anything a consumer can see has been replicated to every in-sync replica, so no surviving failover can take it away.

The visible cost is latency: a record is produced, and only becomes readable one replication round-trip later. That gap is not a bug, it is the durability guarantee showing itself.

ISR — the set that actually decides everything

The in-sync replica set is the replicas currently keeping up with the leader. A follower is in the ISR while it has fetched up to the leader's LEO within replica.lag.time.max.ms (default 30 s). Fall behind for longer — GC pause, slow disk, saturated network, a restart — and the leader removes it. Catch up again and it is re-added.

The ISR is not a static list and it is not Replicas. Almost every durability question comes down to how large is the ISR at the moment of failure, which is why IsrShrinksPerSec is a metric worth alerting on rather than merely graphing.

acks and min.insync.replicas — read them as a pair

acks is a producer setting; min.insync.replicas is a topic/broker setting. Neither is sufficient alone, and the single most common durability misconfiguration is setting one and assuming the other.

acksLeader responds whenLost if
0Immediately — the producer does not waitAnything at all. A network blip loses records silently; there is no retry because there is no response to retry on.
1The leader has written to its own logThe leader dies before a follower replicates. The write was acknowledged and is gone — a silent loss, invisible to the producer.
allEvery replica in the ISR has the recordOnly if the ISR had shrunk far enough first. This is the whole point below.
Why acks=all alone guarantees nothing

acks=all means "all replicas in the ISR", not "all replicas". If two of three followers fall behind and drop out, the ISR is just the leader — and acks=all is then satisfied by the leader alone. It has silently degraded to acks=1, with no error and no warning, at exactly the moment the cluster is least healthy.

min.insync.replicas is the floor that closes this. With min.insync.replicas=2, a producer using acks=all gets NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS when the ISR drops below two — the write is rejected rather than silently under-replicated. You have chosen to fail loudly instead of losing data quietly.

The standard safe configuration, and the reasoning behind each number:

replication.factor   = 3     # tolerate one failure and still have a choice
min.insync.replicas  = 2     # a write needs leader + one follower
acks                 = all   # producer waits for the whole ISR

RF 3 with min ISR 2 survives one broker loss while still accepting writes. Setting min.insync.replicas=3 on RF 3 is a common mistake: it means any single broker failure stops writes entirely, trading all availability for a durability gain you mostly already had.

Worth knowing for a 4.x interview: since Kafka 3.0 the producer defaults are acks=all and enable.idempotence=true (KIP-679). Older answers that say "the default is acks=1" are out of date, and saying so is a cheap way to show the knowledge is current.

Leader epoch, and why truncation used to lose data

When a failed replica rejoins, it may hold records the new leader never had, so it must truncate before it can follow. The question is truncate to where.

Originally a recovering replica truncated to its own high watermark, which created a real divergence window: because the HW propagates to followers one round-trip after it advances, two replicas could disagree about it during a double failure, and committed records could be lost or the logs could silently diverge while both believed they were in sync.

Leader epoch (KIP-101) fixed this. Every leader election increments an epoch number, and each replica keeps a leader-epoch-checkpoint file — you saw it in the lab 01 partition directory — mapping each epoch to the offset where it began. A recovering follower asks the leader "where did epoch N end?" and truncates to exactly that offset, instead of guessing from a watermark that may be stale. Deterministic, and no divergence.

You do not configure this. It is worth knowing because it is the honest answer to "has Kafka ever lost data in normal operation" — yes, before 0.11, through exactly this path.

Unclean leader election

Every in-sync replica is gone and only an out-of-sync one remains. Two choices, no third:

unclean.leader.election.enableBehaviourYou are choosing
false (default)The partition goes offline and stays offline until an in-sync replica returnsConsistency. No acknowledged write is ever lost; the partition is simply unavailable, possibly for a long time.
trueAn out-of-sync replica becomes leader immediatelyAvailability. Writes resume at once, and every record the old leader had beyond this replica's log is permanently gone. Consumers that already read them see the log move backwards.

This is the clearest CAP trade-off Kafka exposes, and the right answer in an interview is not a preference but a per-topic decision. Clickstream or metrics where a gap is survivable and downtime is not: enable it. A payment ledger: never — take the outage. Naming the topic class is what makes the answer senior; "it depends" without naming the dimension is not an answer.

Read the third row as the design intent: Kafka's durability story is to refuse writes rather than to lose them, and every setting above either upholds that or opts out of it.

One failure mode this matrix does not cover: the page cache from M2. Because Kafka does not fsync per record, a simultaneous power loss across the whole ISR can lose data that every replica acknowledged. Replication is the durability mechanism, so correlated failure — one rack, one availability zone, one power domain — is the thing it cannot protect against. That is what broker.rack and rack-aware replica placement are for.

What to watch

MetricMeans
UnderReplicatedPartitionsISR smaller than the replica list. Non-zero is normal briefly during a restart; sustained non-zero is a problem.
UnderMinIsrPartitionCountISR below min.insync.replicas — acks=all producers are being rejected right now. This is the page, not the graph.
IsrShrinksPerSec / IsrExpandsPerSecFlapping ISR: GC pauses, slow disks, network saturation. Usually the early warning before anything else fires.
OfflinePartitionsCountPartitions with no leader at all. Non-zero means data is unavailable.

Also worth knowing: leadership drifts after failures heal, concentrating leaders on whichever brokers came back first. auto.leader.rebalance.enable (default true) periodically returns leadership to the preferred leader — the first entry in the replica list — and kafka-leader-election.sh --election-type preferred forces it. An unbalanced cluster is a throughput problem long before it is an availability one.
